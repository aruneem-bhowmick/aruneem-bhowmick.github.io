Do LLMs know when they're done thinking?

Looking inside large language models (LLMs) for signs that an answer is ready, so they could stop early on easy questions

Aruneem Bhowmick
Mentor: Dr. Seth Polsley · University of Nebraska–Lincoln
[~30 s] Project. When is Transformer Computation Unnecessary? (UCARE 2026–27, mentor Dr. Seth Polsley, UNL). Decoder-only LLMs apply the same depth, every transformer block, to every token regardless of how hard the input is.

Questions. (RQ1) At what depth does task-relevant information become linearly decodable from the residual stream, and does that depth differ by task family? (RQ2) Do deeper blocks add new task information or mainly refine it? (RQ3) Can geometric signals such as layer-to-layer stability flag unnecessary computation? (RQ4) Can halting on those signals beat output-space heuristics (softmax entropy, max-probability confidence) on the compute–accuracy tradeoff?

Figure. A stack of blocks with a read-out after each one: the core measurement of the project.

A quick experiment

Answer each question in your head before the answer appears.

What's the capital of France?

What's 17 × 23?

Paris.

instantly

17 × 20 = 340
17 × 3 = 51
340 + 51 = 391 … I think?

Which one took more effort?

[~1 min] The two prompts stand in for the two task families in the study: factual recall, a content lookup over stored associations, versus multi-step algorithmic computation, where intermediate results must be composed.

The human contrast motivates input-adaptive computation. The technical analogue: a fixed-depth transformer spends the same forward-pass compute per token on both prompts. Keep the claim at the level of effort, not mechanism; nothing here says LLMs solve problems the way people do.

The problem

Your brain adjusts its effort to the question: almost none for Paris, real work for 17 × 23. Today's LLMs don't. They spend exactly the same amount of work on both.

[~30 s] In a standard decoder-only transformer, per-token inference cost is fixed by the architecture: every token passes through all L blocks, roughly 2 × (parameter count) FLOPs per token for the forward pass. There is no built-in way to spend less on easy inputs.

Prior work on adaptive computation: Adaptive Computation Time (Graves, 2016); Universal Transformers with dynamic halting (Dehghani et al., 2019); early-exit encoders such as DeeBERT (Xin et al., 2020); CALM, confidence-based early exit for generation (Schuster et al., 2022); Mixture-of-Depths token routing (Raposo et al., 2024).

The bar chart is schematic, not measured.

How an LLM answers a question

An LLM answers a question the way an automatic car wash cleans a car: everything goes through the same fixed line of steps. A spotless car and a muddy one get every brush and rinse, and an easy question and a hard one get every step.

[~45 s] One bay = one transformer block. In Pythia (GPT-NeoX), each block applies layer norm, computes attention and an MLP in parallel from the same input (parallel residual), and adds both back into the residual stream. Block counts: Pythia-70M 6, 160M 12, 410M 24, 1B 16, 2.8B 32.

What is measured. The residual stream after the embedding layer (index 0) and after every block, giving L + 1 snapshots per input.

Clicks. (1) "needs none of it!" (2) The spotless car leaves after two bays: early exit, halting once the representation already supports the answer. (3) The muddy car takes the full tunnel: the fixed-depth baseline every input gets today.

The cost

Source: International Energy Agency, Energy and AI (April 2025): 415 TWh in 2024, about 945 TWh projected for 2030.

The models I studied have 6 to 32 steps, and every question pays for all of them. Data centers already use about 1.5% of the world's electricity, and that could double by 2030.

[~45 s] Per-token compute scales linearly with depth, so exiting after k of L blocks saves up to roughly (L − k)/L of the forward pass, minus the cost of the halting check. In autoregressive generation, early exit also has to handle the KV cache for skipped blocks, which later tokens still attend to; CALM propagates hidden states to fill them in (Schuster et al., 2022).

How humans think

Fast

  • Recognizing a face
  • Recalling a capital city
  • Hearing that someone is upset

Slow

  • Long multiplication
  • Planning a route
  • Checking a tricky argument

Psychologist Daniel Kahneman describes two speeds of human thinking: fast and automatic, or slow and deliberate (Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011). an LLM isn't a brain, but matching effort to difficulty makes sense for both.

[~1 min] Kahneman's System 1 / System 2 (Thinking, Fast and Slow, 2011) is used only as a motivating analogy.

Technical counterpart. The hypothesised split is between tasks whose answer is a lookup over stored associations (factual recall, sentiment) and tasks that compose intermediate results (modular arithmetic, bracket matching). Interpretability work supports the lookup side: feed-forward layers act as key–value memories (Geva et al., 2021), and factual associations can be localised to and edited in mid-layer MLPs (Meng et al., 2022, ROME).

No claim of cognitive equivalence.

My hypothesis

My bet: easy questions are "done" early. If the LLM already has the answer halfway down the line, the rest of the line is wasted work. Could it stop there?

[~30 s] H1 (central). Factual-recall and shallow-semantic tasks become linearly decodable at earlier layers than algorithmic reasoning tasks: recall is a lookup that finishes mid-depth, while reasoning needs further composition in deeper blocks.

Supporting hypotheses. H2: mutual information between representations and targets plateaus early for lookup and keeps rising for reasoning. H3: layer-to-layer representation stability converges earlier for lookup. H4: stability-based halting matches entropy-based early exit in accuracy with less compute.

The dimmed stations in the top row are the computation H1 predicts is unnecessary for lookup inputs.

Looking inside

To see what the LLM knows, I pause it after every step and check its work so far, like stopping a student midway through a problem to ask: "Do you know it yet?"

[~45 s] Extraction. Forward hooks on the embedding layer and on each GPT-NeoX block capture the residual stream. Weights are frozen and the model is never modified (inference only). Each input is pooled to one vector per layer: last-token pooling for single-sequence tasks; for MRPC the two sentences are pooled separately and concatenated. Vectors are cached as safetensors with a per-example manifest.

Probing. A probe is trained independently at each layer, giving a score-versus-depth curve per task.

Prediction depth (Baldock et al., 2021) is also computed per example: the earliest layer from which the probe's prediction never changes again. It is the per-example version of "when did it know?"

Honesty check #1

To read the LLM's work, I use a deliberately simple detector: a highlighter, not a detective. If something this simple can find the answer, the answer was plainly there.

[~30 s] The probe is linear: L2-regularised logistic regression for classification, ridge regression for the periodic-table task. Features are standardised and classes balanced. Regularisation strength is chosen from a grid inside 5-fold stratified cross-validation.

Why linear (Alain & Bengio, 2016). An expressive probe such as an MLP can learn the task from the features on its own, which mixes up what the representation encodes with what the probe computes (Hewitt & Liang, 2019). "Linearly decodable at layer l" is therefore the working definition of "the answer is already there." The cost is that information present but non-linearly encoded is missed, so these are lower bounds.

Honesty check #2

Then comes the placebo test. Every check is rerun with the answers scrambled, and like a drug trial, the real result has to beat the placebo.

[~45 s] Control. A shuffled-label baseline: same features, same folds, same probe, but the labels randomly permuted across examples. Selectivity = task score − control score, a simpler variant of Hewitt & Liang's control tasks (2019). It measures how much of the probe's score reflects real structure rather than the probe's capacity to fit arbitrary labels.

Limitation. Shuffling breaks every feature–label relationship, real or spurious. A shortcut that is genuinely correlated with the label survives in the real run and is absent from the control, so it inflates selectivity instead of being caught. That is the setup for slide 17.

What I tested

I tested four kinds of questions: two I expected the LLM to answer fast, and two puzzles I expected to need the whole line.

[~20 s] Track A tasks by family.
Factual lookup: LAMA/T-REx (Petroni et al., 2019), a relation-filtered subset; and a periodic-table property task (regression).
Shallow semantic: SST-2 sentiment; MRPC paraphrase detection.
Algorithmic: Dyck-3 well-formedness; modular addition mod 7.

Lookup and semantic are predicted "fast", algorithmic "slow". Synthetic tasks come from seeded generators, so each of the five seeds is a fresh dataset rather than a resample.

Slow questions need step-by-step work, like counting around a clock or checking that every bracket closes in the right order. I expected these to take the whole line.

[~30 s] Clock math = modular addition, (a + b) mod p. The pilot uses p = 7; the 12-hour clock on the slide is the same operation with a familiar modulus. It is a standard interpretability testbed because networks trained on it learn a recognisable circular (Fourier) representation (Nanda et al., 2023).

Bracket puzzle = Dyck-3: is a sequence over three bracket types well-formed? It requires tracking a stack of open brackets and is a canonical formal-language task for studying hierarchical structure in transformers (Hahn, 2020; Yao et al., 2021). Brackets are space-separated so each is a single token.

Finding 1

Best step, 160M-parameter Pythia model, average of 5 repeats. Placebo = same test with the answers shuffled.

The answer really is in there. On every kind of question, the real result clears the placebo, so the LLM's work in progress genuinely contains the answer.

[~45 s] Pythia-160M, best layer per task, mean of 5 seeds, 5-fold CV. Real vs placebo:
Trivia 0.867 vs 0.373 (chance 1/3); sentiment 0.742 vs 0.507 (1/2); brackets 0.685 vs 0.509 (1/2); clock math 0.981 vs 0.145 (1/7).

Placebo bars at chance confirm the control behaves as designed. Selectivity is large for trivia (0.49) and clock math (0.84), small for sentiment (0.24) and brackets (0.18). MRPC, not shown, is weakest (0.12 on 160M).

Caveat. A positive gap shows the information is linearly decodable, not that the model uses it. Causal claims need interventions such as ablation or activation patching.

Finding 2

Pythia 70M (6 steps) and 160M (12 steps), average of 5 repeats (they agree within about 2 points).

Clock math has an "aha" moment: the LLM starts at pure guessing, then suddenly gets it. The bigger model gets there 17% of the way through, the smaller one a third to half.

[~45 s] Modular addition mod 7. Probe accuracy against relative depth (block index ÷ number of blocks), so both models share one axis. Both start near chance at the embedding layer and plateau at about 0.98. Pythia-160M reaches the plateau by about block 2 of 12 (17% depth); 70M needs a third to half of its depth. Selectivity is about 0.83 for both. Means of 5 seeds; the two models agree within about 2 points at the plateau.

Not grokking. This is a jump over depth inside a trained model. Grokking (Power et al., 2022) is a delayed jump over training time.

Caveat. Early decodability means the answer is linearly present, not that later blocks are unnecessary for the model's own output. Testing that is the job of the halting experiments.

Finding 3: a surprise

Meet Clever Hans, a horse who seemed to do arithmetic by tapping his hoof. He was really reading his trainer's body language, and stopped tapping when the trainer relaxed.

[~30 s] Clever Hans (Pfungst, 1911) is the standard name for a model succeeding through a spurious cue rather than the intended skill, also called shortcut learning (Geirhos et al., 2020).

Probing analogue. The probe reads a surface feature correlated with the label (length, token identity, position) instead of the computed property. Such features are often linearly decodable at the embedding layer, so layer-0 accuracy is a useful sanity check: a task that needs computation should be at chance before any block has run.

Finding 3, continued

My bracket puzzle had a Clever Hans: broken examples often ended in a telltale bracket, so the detector could spot them without solving anything. I redesigned the puzzle so the ending can't give it away, and I'm re-running it now.

[~45 s] Symptom. Pilot v1 found Dyck-3 decodable at about 57% at layer 0, before any block had run. For a task that needs stack tracking, above-chance layer-0 accuracy points to a surface cue.

Suspected cause and fix. The original corruption (swap, delete or insert a bracket) could change sequence length and per-type bracket counts, and the BPE tokenizer merged runs of brackets so the final token could span several characters. The fix permutes interior brackets only, verifies the result is malformed, preserves length and the bracket multiset, and space-separates brackets so each is one token.

Rerun (5 seeds, both models): layer-0 accuracy 0.573 → 0.559; best-layer selectivity 0.156 → 0.161 (70M) and 0.176 → 0.190 (160M). The fix barely moved the numbers, so the remaining layer-0 signal has a source not yet identified. Open item.

Where things stand

The results are real in places, but not yet clean. With small models and early data, the "fast vs. slow" pattern shows up, though not as neatly as I bet. That's what the next stage is for.

[~45 s] Status against H1 at pilot scale (70M and 160M only):
Factual and semantic tasks are linearly decodable with positive selectivity, but not uniformly earlier than algorithmic ones.
Modular arithmetic, an algorithmic task, becomes decodable earliest and most cleanly: the opposite of H1's prediction.
LAMA/T-REx peaks late (final block on 160M).
Periodic-table regression is non-monotonic across depth, partly from ill-conditioned ridge fits at low regularisation (its grid was raised to 0.1–100).
Dyck-3 stays low-selectivity after the corruption fix.

H2 to H4 are not yet tested. Two models is too few to separate task effects from scale effects.

Why it matters

Cheaper, greener LLMs

Easy questions stop early, saving electricity and time.

LLMs on everyday devices

Less work per answer means it can run on a phone, not only in a data center.

A warning light

If a question keeps the LLM working to the very end, maybe a human should double-check.

If LLMs could stop when they're sure, all three of these become possible, and the signal for when to stop comes from inside the LLM itself.

[~1.5 min] Cost. Early exit cuts per-token compute; CALM reports up to about 3× speedups on generation tasks using output-confidence exits (Schuster et al., 2022).
Devices. Average depth, not maximum depth, drives latency and energy, which matters for on-device inference.
Warning light. An input that keeps the model computing to the final block, or whose representation never stabilises, may be a useful uncertainty signal for selective prediction or human review.

Contribution. The signal itself: representation-geometric stopping criteria instead of softmax-entropy or max-probability thresholds, which are often miscalibrated in modern networks (Guo et al., 2017).
